# Memex, the idea (1945)

[Português](memex.pt-BR.md) · [Brain](../README.md) · Next leap: [internet](internet.md)

**In one sentence:** in 1945 Vannevar Bush imagined a personal machine to keep everything a person reads, linked by trails of association; nearly every information leap since then has realized a piece of that idea.

## What it was

Bush coordinated United States scientific research during the Second World War. When it ended, he asked what scientists should do next, and answered in an essay in The Atlantic, July 1945: "As We May Think". The problem, for him, was that humanity produced knowledge faster than it could find and use it.

The answer was the **Memex**, a desk of microfilm where a person would keep books, notes and letters, and make **trails**: links from one document to another, the way the mind associates. Bush himself foresaw: "Wholly new forms of encyclopedias will appear, ready-made with a mesh of associative trails running through them."

## What he foresaw and what came

| Bush's idea | Who realized it | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linking documents by association | hypertext and the [web](web.md) | 1989 to 1993 |
| Encyclopedias with ready-made trails | Wikipedia | 2001 |
| A machine that augments its user's thinking | Doug Engelbart, "Augmenting Human Intellect" (1962) and the demonstration of 9 December 1968 | 1962 to 1968 |
| Someone who keeps the trails current as the collection grows | **nobody, until language models** | now |

## Why it matters for this brain

The last row of the table is why this brain exists. Bush's trails were made by hand, and keeping links by hand is the work everyone abandons. A language model reads, links, flags contradictions and updates without tiring. The pattern is described in detail in Andrej Karpathy's "llm-wiki", which names the Memex as its ancestor: "The part he couldn't solve was who does the maintenance."
